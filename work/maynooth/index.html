Furniture commerce · Responsive web

Maynooth Furniture

Helping customers discover affordable, high-end furniture and make it their own—with clearer information and easier access to designers.

My roleUX Designer
Duration3 months
FocusResearch · Interaction · Prototyping
01 · The challenge

Buying furniture without seeing it in your home.

Maynooth is a furniture-commerce concept focused on affordable high-end and personalized design. The project aimed to improve information architecture, make customization easier to access, and attract new customers.

Buying furniture online means making decisions without touching it or seeing every detail. Choosing the wrong item—and dealing with a return—can be daunting. I focused on shoppers who value quality, convenience, and furniture that reflects their individual taste.

Communication

How could we make it easier for customers to reach furniture-company staff?

Customization

How could we give customers simple access to personalized furniture, with expert advice?

02 · Research & persona

Understanding what makes a purchase feel right.

The three-month process moved through research, definition, design and prototyping, feedback, and reflection. It included a review of past research, competitive analysis, interviews, affinity mapping, user flows, wireframes, and usability testing.

Interview goals

Understand reasons for returns and exchanges, demand for access to staff, and frustrations with product descriptions.

Questions

What worries people when shopping online? How do online purchases compare with local stores? Which features are essential?

Katherine · 50 · Executive · Orlando, Florida

“I now have the time & resources to express my individual style in our home.”

Katherine is married, has children at university, and earns $110,000 a year at an international medical-device company. Both style- and price-conscious, she is gradually updating her home. She gathers ideas on Pinterest and wants distinctive furniture, but finds it difficult to choose pieces that work with her space.

Interview themes included difficulty reaching experts, a desire for personalization, frustrating returns, limited payment methods, unclear care instructions, installation concerns, and the motivation of rewards.

03 · Defining the MVP

Four priorities for a more useful shopping experience.

Affinity mapping and sketching helped turn the research into a focused set of features.

Recommendations

Furniture suggestions and style guides help customers find a direction.

Access to staff

A clear path to service before and after a purchase.

Customization

A dedicated space to work with designers and adapt a piece to personal taste.

Points & rewards

Purchase-based points and rewards recognize progress and encourage return visits.

Information architecture & user flow

The structure organized product dimensions, materials, care, related items, style tips, customization, and help. The main flow connected search, item selection, wishlists, color customization, staff chat, the shopping bag, and checkout.

04 · Wireframes

Making the whole journey visible.

Early wireframes connected discovery, account creation, category and product pages, wishlists, search, customization, and checkout.

05 · Testing & prioritization

Three gaps changed the design.

Five participants aged 35–55 joined remote sessions lasting 30–40 minutes. Testing included four tasks and nine follow-up questions: browse and search, add an item to a wishlist, customize a color and speak with staff, then view the bag and check out.

Organize wishlists

Participants needed separate lists or boards for different types of furniture. I added a popup for creating boards.

Choose the right help

A general chat entry was not enough. I added distinct options for speaking with a designer or a staff member.

See it in context

It was difficult to imagine an item at home. I explored uploading a room photo and placing furniture within it.

Feature prioritization helped identify low-value, high-effort ideas and clarify shortcomings in the initial design.

06 · Final design

A clearer route from inspiration to a personal choice.

Homepage & discovery

The homepage prioritizes important sections, organizes categories, and keeps helpful information easy to find. The footer uses a clean layout and a clear company identity.

Category & product pages

Category pages provide more specific furniture types and filters, with names, designers, and prices. Product pages combine photography, measurements, materials, related pieces, style tips, and buyer opinions to support a more informed choice.

Customization

Customers can adapt the original furniture design with support from designers and staff, and explore how a piece could look in their own space.

07 · Reflection

Prioritizing the features that matter.

User interviews were central to this project. I learned that participants placed particular importance on furniture design and quality, beyond delivery and installation services. User flows and prioritization exercises helped me analyze the problems and decide what to include.

The hardest part was choosing the priority features and combining them into a coherent design. Reading relevant articles and studying other designers’ work helped me develop the solution.
